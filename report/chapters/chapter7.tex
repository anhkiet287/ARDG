\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{chap:conclusion}

\section{Summary of Contributions}

This report studied cross-attack adversarial robustness: the problem that a
model trained to resist one adversarial threat may not resist another threat
with a different norm, objective, or optimization behavior.  The central
motivation was not simply to raise one robustness score, but to understand how
robustness is distributed across attack families and to develop and test a
training mechanism aimed at improving this distribution under a controlled
experimental protocol.  In this framing, attack-induced examples behave like domain-like
groups, and a training procedure should be evaluated by how well it balances
these groups rather than by how strongly it specializes to one source attack.

The methodological contribution is a progression from Multi-AT to AttackDRO and
then to AttackDRO++.  Multi-AT provides the uniform multi-attack baseline by
training on PGD-$\ell_\infty$ and DDN-$\ell_2$ source attacks without adaptive
group weighting.  AttackDRO adds group DRO over attack identities, which makes
the training objective sensitive to source-attack difficulty.  AttackDRO++
extends this idea by replacing fixed attack identities with discovered latent
groups, using augmented clustering features that include representations,
labels, and gradient fingerprints.  This lets the grouping mechanism capture
hard regions that may cut across the original attack labels.

The final method also includes a stabilization mechanism: the adaptive
Cluster-DRO objective is anchored to the uniform multi-attack objective.  This
uniform anchor prevents group weighting from fully replacing the Multi-AT
training signal, while still allowing difficult discovered groups to receive
additional emphasis.  The resulting AttackDRO++ configuration is therefore best
understood as a refinement of Multi-AT rather than as a separate replacement for
multi-attack training.

The contribution of this work should be read at two connected levels:
methodologically, AttackDRO++ introduces a group-aware multi-attack training
mechanism built from latent cluster discovery, gradient fingerprints, and a
uniform anchor; empirically, the report tests whether that mechanism improves
robustness balance under the fixed CIFAR-10 and ResNet-18 protocol.  The
empirical claim is therefore narrower than the method itself: the evidence
supports an improvement in robustness balance in the evaluated CIFAR-10 and
ResNet-18 setting, not a general claim of robustness across datasets,
architectures, or attack configurations.  The report evaluates this claim
through aggregate whitebox robustness, per-attack breakdowns, per-class and
attack-by-class drilldowns, graybox transfer, statistical comparisons, and
hyperparameter sensitivity studies.  It does not aim to establish universal
robustness or broad architecture generalization; instead, it tests whether
group-aware multi-attack training can improve robustness balance under a fixed
and reproducible experimental protocol.

The first two answers support the premise of the report.  Single-attack
training remains useful when the target threat family is known, but it creates a
specialist profile that leaves the complementary threat family exposed.
Multi-attack training reduces this specialization by covering both source
threat geometries, yet the Chapter~\ref{chap:results_analysis} drilldowns show
that some attack families and CIFAR-10 classes remain systematically harder
than others even under uniform averaging.

The third answer requires the most careful reading because its strength varies
across the evidence panels.  AttackDRO++ improves average
cross-attack balance and reduces seed-to-seed variation relative to Multi-AT in
the main whitebox panel.  This answer is strongest for the aggregate and
held-out whitebox metrics, where paired differences are statistically supported
with medium effect sizes.  It is weakest for the hardest-case metrics
(Worst(8) and full-test AutoAttack-$\ell_\infty$), where AttackDRO++ is
statistically indistinguishable from Multi-AT and should be read as broadly
comparable rather than as an improvement.  Graybox transfer supports a positive
trend over Multi-AT, while also showing that transfer accuracy can be inflated
by specialist geometry mismatch.  The WRN-28-10 check in
Appendix~\ref{app:extras} is supplementary only: it is a single-run result that
is useful mainly because it prevents the conclusion from becoming stronger than
the evidence supports.  Taken together, the RQ3 evidence supports a bounded
improvement in average robustness balance under the evaluated protocol, not a
claim that transfers across architectures or attack configurations.

The fourth answer indicates that the selected configuration is not highly
sensitive to the tested hyperparameter ranges.  However, because the ablation
panels are smaller than the main aggregate panel, they should be read as
practical configuration guidance rather than as evidence that the configuration
was exhaustively optimized.

\section{Limitations of the Current Study}

These limitations define the boundary of the current evidence rather than
invalidating the result; they mark where the experimental scope ends and where
future work must extend the evaluation before broader claims can be made.

The most immediate boundary is dataset scope.  The main experiments use CIFAR-10, which
is a standard benchmark for adversarial training but still a small,
low-resolution image dataset.  The observed behavior may not transfer directly
to CIFAR-100, Tiny ImageNet, ImageNet-scale data, or non-image domains.  Larger
datasets may change both the difficulty of the attack suite and the usefulness
of cluster-based group discovery, particularly if the number of meaningful
latent groups scales with dataset complexity.

Architecture scope is a related constraint.  The main evidence uses ResNet-18,
so the central empirical claims are bounded to that backbone.
Appendix~\ref{app:extras} includes a WRN-28-10 architecture check, but it is a
single-run supplementary experiment and does not support a broad
architecture-level claim.  The mixed supplementary architecture evidence is
useful because it prevents the conclusion from being stronger than the data
support, but it also means that architecture generalization remains an open
question.

The evaluation is also bounded by its attack suite.  The evaluation covers a fixed set
of whitebox and graybox attacks, with AutoAttack-$\ell_\infty$ reported
separately as a standardized stress test.  This does not establish certified
robustness, full adaptive robustness, or robustness against every possible
attack implementation.  In particular, an attacker aware of the grouping
mechanism could design adaptive tests that are not covered by the present
protocol.

Statistical power varies across the evidence panels.  The main aggregate
comparisons use 20 paired random-seed evaluations, but the class-wise analysis
uses five seeds, the graybox analysis uses a focused surrogate panel, and the
ablation studies use three runs per setting.  These smaller panels are
sufficient to identify trends and failure modes, but they carry less statistical
power than the main paired aggregate comparisons and should be interpreted
accordingly.

The method's own complexity is a practical constraint.  AttackDRO++ depends
on feature banks, clustering quality, gradient-fingerprint construction, and a
reasonable anchor strength.  These components make the method more expressive
than uniform multi-attack training, but they also add implementation and tuning
requirements that may limit practical adoption.  The ablations suggest that the
selected configuration is stable in the evaluated setting, not that every
grouping choice will behave equally well in other settings.

\section{Directions for Future Work}

Future work should first test whether the same behavior holds across broader
datasets and architectures, because these are the primary boundaries of the
current evidence.  The
current evidence should be repeated on CIFAR-100, Tiny ImageNet, and larger
image datasets, where class structure and feature complexity are richer.  The
architecture check should also be expanded across ResNet variants, WRN
backbones, and possibly transformer-style models so that the interaction
between model capacity and group-aware training can be measured systematically.

Future evaluation should also include stronger and more adaptive attacks.  This
includes larger attack suites, additional norms, stronger AutoAttack settings,
and attacks that explicitly account for the grouping mechanism.  Such evaluation
would test whether the robustness balance observed here survives when the
attacker is not limited to the fixed protocol used in
Chapter~\ref{chap:experimental_setup}.

The grouping mechanism itself is another direction for improvement.  AttackDRO++
uses K-means over augmented features, but future work could explore learned
group discovery, soft assignments, more stable online clustering, or
alternative gradient fingerprints.  These variants may reduce sensitivity to
feature-bank quality and allow the grouping structure to adapt more smoothly
during training.

Graybox and blackbox transfer should also be expanded.  The current graybox
analysis uses independently trained checkpoints from the same method families.
Future work could add more surrogate architectures, cross-architecture transfer,
larger surrogate ensembles, and transfer from methods not used during training.
This would clarify whether group-aware multi-attack training improves robustness
only within a controlled family of models or also under broader deployment-like
transfer conditions.

Finally, future work should reduce the computational overhead of the method.
Feature banks, gradient fingerprints, cluster refreshes, and additional
bookkeeping all increase training complexity.  More efficient feature updates,
lower-cost fingerprint approximations, and adaptive refresh schedules would make
AttackDRO++ more practical for larger models and datasets.

\section{Closing Remarks}

Cross-attack robustness is not reducible to a single attack score; it depends
on how robustness is distributed across threat geometries,
classes, and evaluation conditions.  Under the CIFAR-10 and ResNet-18 protocol
used in this report, AttackDRO++ supports the view that group-aware
multi-attack training can improve robustness balance over a uniform multi-attack
baseline.  The improvement is clearest on average and held-out robustness
metrics; the hardest-case and AutoAttack-$\ell_\infty$ behavior is statistically
indistinguishable from Multi-AT and does not meaningfully degrade in the
evaluated panel, but it does not improve either.  The results remain
conditional: broader datasets, stronger adaptive attacks, additional
architectures, and larger transfer settings are all needed before making
general claims.

The main conclusion is therefore not that AttackDRO++ establishes universal
robustness, but that adversarial robustness is better evaluated and optimized
as a multi-domain problem than as a single-attack score.
