\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

\section{Motivation}

Deep neural networks can achieve high standard accuracy while remaining
sensitive to carefully constructed perturbations of their inputs.  The
perturbations studied in adversarial robustness are usually constrained to be
small under a chosen norm, yet they can change the prediction of a trained
classifier with high reliability \cite{szegedy2014intriguing,goodfellow2015explaining}.
Adversarial training responds to this failure mode by including adversarial
examples during optimization, and it has become one of the most widely used
empirical defenses under specified threat models \cite{madry2019towards}.

The strength of adversarial training is also the source of a practical
limitation: robustness is shaped by the attack used to generate the training
examples.  A model trained primarily against a projected-gradient
$\ell_\infty$ attack may learn invariances that are useful for that geometry
without transferring equally well to $\ell_2$ attacks, decision-boundary
attacks, or attacks with different optimization behavior.  Conversely, a model
trained against an $\ell_2$ source attack may show a different pattern of
robustness and weakness.  This report studies that mismatch as a
cross-attack robustness problem, where the relevant question is not whether a
model survives one attack, but how its robust accuracy is distributed across a
suite of seen and held-out attacks.

Multi-attack adversarial training is a natural stronger baseline because it
exposes the model to more than one source attack during training.  In this
report, Multi-AT denotes uniform multi-attack adversarial training over shared
source attacks.  This design broadens the training signal, but it still treats
all source examples through a fixed averaging rule.  If the hardest examples
cut across attack families, classes, and optimization trajectories, then fixed
attack identities can be too coarse to describe where training is failing.

Group distributionally robust optimization (Group DRO) offers a complementary
view: rather than optimizing only the average loss, training can adaptively
emphasize groups with higher loss \cite{sagawa2019groupdro}.  Its usefulness
depends on whether the groups correspond to meaningful weaknesses.  AttackDRO
uses fixed source attack identities as groups, but those identities do not
necessarily capture latent hard subsets within an attack family or shared
failure modes across families.  This motivates a move from known attack labels
to discovered latent groups.

Domain generalization provides a useful lens for this move.  Attacks can be
viewed as algorithm-induced domains because each attack changes the input
distribution according to a particular perturbation set, loss objective, and
optimization procedure.  At the same time, adversarial robustness is not the
same problem as ordinary domain generalization: evaluation is threat-model
aware, attacks are constructed against the model, and robustness claims must be
conditioned on the attacks that were actually tested.  For that reason, this
report uses the attacks-as-domains perspective as a design tool, not as a claim
that the final method solves general domain generalization.

The final method in this report, AttackDRO++, combines latent cluster discovery
with optimization-aware information.  Cluster-DRO first replaces fixed attack
identities with discovered latent clusters.  AttackDRO++ then augments this
cluster-level grouping with gradient fingerprints and a uniform anchor.  The
gradient fingerprints help clustering reflect optimization-level difficulty,
while the anchor keeps adaptive reweighting tied to the stable Multi-AT
training signal.  The resulting method is a uniform-anchored Cluster-DRO
procedure with gradient fingerprints.

The empirical claim is deliberately bounded.  In the main CIFAR-10 and
ResNet-18 setting, AttackDRO++ increases average robustness balance relative
to the uniform multi-attack baseline, while hardest-case robustness, full-test
AutoAttack, graybox transfer, ablation behavior, and supplementary WRN-28-10
checks are interpreted cautiously.  The report therefore focuses on a practical
multi-attack adversarial training setting rather than unrestricted robustness
against arbitrary attacks.

\section{Problem Statement}

This report studies robust image classification under a controlled multi-attack
training protocol.  A classifier is trained using adversarial examples from a
fixed source attack set, with PGD-$\ell_\infty$ and DDN-$\ell_2$ serving as
the shared sources for the multi-attack methods.  The trained classifier is
then evaluated across seen and held-out attacks that differ in perturbation
norm, optimization objective, and attack dynamics.  The central problem is how
to allocate training emphasis so that robustness is balanced across this
evaluation suite without optimizing directly for held-out attacks.

Uniform multi-attack training is the starting point because it controls for
the source adversarial data generation process.  This report introduces
AttackDRO and Cluster-DRO as intermediate design steps that motivate the final
method.  The main empirical comparison in Chapter~\ref{chap:results_analysis}
focuses on PGD-AT, DDN-AT, Multi-AT, and AttackDRO++ under the fixed
experimental protocol.  Under this setup, fixed attack identities are a
plausible first grouping, but they can miss examples that are hard for reasons
not captured by the source attack label.  A high-loss subset may be defined by
class structure, representation geometry, local margin, optimization trajectory,
or an interaction among these factors.

The problem is therefore not only to train on multiple attacks, but to identify
which parts of the induced training distribution deserve additional emphasis.
Latent cluster discovery addresses the group-definition problem by replacing
manual attack labels with groups inferred from training examples.  Gradient
fingerprints add information about how examples interact with the classifier
head during optimization, so that clustering is not limited to representation
similarity alone.  A uniform anchor then stabilizes the adaptive objective by
retaining a direct connection to the Multi-AT loss.

Evaluation must also reflect the structure of the problem.  Mean(8) summarizes
average robust accuracy across the eight non-AutoAttack attacks, Worst(8)
reports the hardest attack in that suite, and held-out summaries separate
attacks that were not used as training sources.  AutoAttack-$\ell_\infty$ is
reported separately as a hard evaluation rather than folded into Mean(8)
\cite{croce2020reliable}.  The goal is to assess robust average performance
and hardest-case behavior under a predefined protocol, with claims interpreted
through paired multi-seed comparisons.

\section{Research Questions and Objectives}

The report is organized around four research questions that connect the
motivation in this chapter to the empirical answers in
Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}.  The questions move from the behavior of
single-source adversarial training, through the role of uniform multi-attack
training, to the additional value and configuration sensitivity of AttackDRO++.

\textbf{RQ1:} To what extent does single-source adversarial training produce
attack-dependent robustness across attacks with different norms, objectives, or
optimization behavior?

\textbf{RQ2:} Does uniform multi-attack adversarial training improve robustness
balance across seen and held-out attacks compared with single-source
adversarial training baselines?

\textbf{RQ3:} Does AttackDRO++ improve average robustness balance over the
uniform multi-attack baseline while preserving hardest-case and AutoAttack
behavior under the evaluated protocol?

\textbf{RQ4:} Which configuration choices, including cluster count, anchor
strength, and cluster refresh schedule, are most important for stable
AttackDRO++ performance?

These questions lead to four concrete objectives.  The report builds a
controlled multi-attack adversarial training and evaluation pipeline; introduces
AttackDRO and Cluster-DRO as methodology steps that lead to AttackDRO++;
compares PGD-AT, DDN-AT, Multi-AT, and AttackDRO++ in the main Chapter~6
empirical panel; evaluates per-attack accuracy, Mean(8), Worst(8), held-out
attack summaries, AutoAttack-$\ell_\infty$, graybox transfer, ablations, and
supplementary architecture checks; and interprets empirical claims
conservatively through paired multi-seed comparisons.

\section{Contributions}

This report makes five contributions within the bounded setting of CIFAR-10
robust training and the protocol defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:experimental_setup}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item It frames cross-attack robustness as a practical multi-attack training
problem and defines a metric protocol that separates per-attack accuracy,
Mean(8), Worst(8), held-out attacks, and AutoAttack-$\ell_\infty$ stress
evaluation.

\item It provides a controlled main comparison of single-source adversarial
training, Multi-AT, and AttackDRO++ under shared source attacks, while using
AttackDRO and Cluster-DRO as intermediate design steps to keep the distinction
between adversarial data generation and group-weighting design explicit.

\item It defines AttackDRO++ as the final proposed method: uniform-anchored
Cluster-DRO with gradient fingerprints.  The method combines latent cluster
discovery, augmented clustering features, optimization-aware gradient
fingerprints, and a uniform anchor in one training pipeline.

\item It evaluates the method over a multi-seed CIFAR-10 and ResNet-18 panel,
including whitebox attack summaries, seen and held-out attack breakdowns,
paired statistical comparisons, class-wise diagnostics, and graybox transfer
analysis.

\item It analyzes design choices through ablations and supplementary checks,
including cluster count, anchor strength, refresh schedule, AutoAttack
evaluation, and a WRN-28-10 architecture check, while keeping the resulting
claims tied to the evidence available for each setting.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Report Organization}

Chapter~\ref{chap:background} introduces the technical background for the
report, including adversarial examples, attack algorithms, adversarial
training, distributionally robust optimization, domain generalization,
clustering, and the statistical tools used for paired multi-seed evaluation.
Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms} contains compact pseudocode for the attack and
training procedures so that Chapter~\ref{chap:background} can remain
conceptual rather than algorithm-heavy.

Chapter~\ref{chap:related_work} reviews related work on multi-attack
adversarial training, Group DRO, domain generalization, and cluster discovery.
It uses those strands to identify the remaining gap that motivates a method
combining multi-attack training, adaptive group emphasis, latent group
discovery, optimization-aware clustering, and uniform anchoring.

Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} defines AttackDRO++ and the training progression
leading to it: Multi-AT, AttackDRO, Cluster-DRO, and the final uniform-anchored
Cluster-DRO method with gradient fingerprints.  Chapter~\ref{chap:experimental_setup}
then specifies the experimental setup, including dataset, architecture, source
attacks, evaluation suite, metrics, hyperparameters, statistical protocol, and
graybox transfer design.

Chapter~\ref{chap:results_analysis} presents the empirical analysis: main
whitebox results, class-wise whitebox diagnostics, graybox transfer, sensitivity
studies, and supplementary checks.  Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} closes the
report by answering the research questions, stating the limitations of the
evidence, and outlining extensions that would be needed to test the method
under broader datasets, architectures, and attack settings.
